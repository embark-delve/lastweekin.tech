AI agents get their own phone directory built atop DNS

The Register AI

DNS‑AID is an open‑source project that lets AI agents discover one another through the existing Domain Name System, eliminating the need for custom registries or hard‑coded ports. Built on DNS features such as SVCB (and TXT fallback) plus optional DNSSEC, DANE TLSA, and JWS signatures, it enables agents to publish and locate services by name, function, or domain via records like _{agent‑name}._{protocol}._agents.{domain}. The Linux Foundation will govern the project, which was originally developed by Infoblox, and is already supported by major DNS providers (AWS, Cloudflare, Azure, Google, etc.). Developers can get started by installing the dns‑aid tool, initializing a zone, publishing an SVCB record, and optionally signing with DNSSEC. By leveraging DNS’s proven global scalability and ubiquity, DNS‑AID offers a “web‑native” routing layer that could underpin a future $3‑$5 trillion agent‑to‑agent economy.

Best Sleep Trackers of 2026: Oura, Whoop, and Eight Sleep

WIRED General Tech

Apple’s own smartwatch remains the most affordable option: the Series 11 ($399) can track four sleep stages using its heart‑rate sensor and accelerometer, and newer models add blood‑oxygen and temperature readings, but it offers no overall sleep score. Garmin’s premium Epix Pro ($1,000) delivers advanced sleep monitoring—stages, SpO₂, respiratory rate and restlessness—but tends to over‑estimate sleep duration and ignores naps in its score. Google’s Nest Hub Max ($229) uses radar and a microphone to monitor sleep without a wearable, yet it consistently overestimates REM and misses wake periods, while the cheaper second‑gen model ($100) offers similar performance. Headband contenders like Muse S Athena ($475) provide EEG‑style brain data plus heart‑rate and respiration, yet can be uncomfortable and prone to failure if removed during the night. Withings’ ScanWatch 2 ($370) offers a full four‑stage profile with respiratory‑rate PPG and additional metrics, though its bulk may disturb sleep and it can misclassify quiet wakefulness. The company’s Sleep Tracking Mat ($200) sits under the mattress and gathers motion, breathing, heart‑rate and snoring data, but its assumption that stillness equals sleep can skew scores and it needs a power outlet. Finally, the low‑cost Sleep Routine app ($60 / yr) gives nightly stage reports with reasonable accuracy but is occasionally buggy. Each device trades off price, comfort and data depth, so the best choice depends on whether you prioritize convenience, comprehensive metrics or affordability.
