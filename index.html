Blue Origin's rocket reuse achievement marred by upper stage failure

Ars Technica AI

Blue Origin’s third New Glenn launch marked the first successful reflight of an orbital‑class booster, but the mission ended with a setback for the company’s flagship rocket, which is slated to carry NASA’s Artemis crew to the moon. The 321‑ft vehicle lifted off from Cape Canaveral at 7:25 am EDT, the BE‑4 methane engines powered the lift‑off and the first‑stage booster shed its engines after three minutes, separating from the upper stage that used BE‑3U liquid hydrogen/oxygen engines. The booster followed a parabolic trajectory, re‑ignited for two braking burns, and landed safely on a ship in the Atlantic, becoming the second flight of the “Never Tell Me The Odds” booster. While the successful recovery demonstrates progress toward reusable, high‑volume launches, the flight also exposed challenges in New Glenn’s larger, more complex design—highlighting that, unlike SpaceX’s rapid Falcon 9 refurbishment, Blue Origin still has hurdles to overcome before it can match the rapid reflight cadence needed for the Artemis program.

The Register AI

Blue Origin’s New Glenn rocket successfully landed its reused first stage on the Jacklyn platform during its April 19 launch, but the mission failed to place AST SpaceMobile’s satellite Bluebird 7 into a usable orbit; the second stage delivered the satellite to a lower‑than‑planned, off‑nominal orbit that its own thrusters could not correct, forcing AST to de‑orbit the payload and recover its cost through insurance. I’ve fired one of America’s most powerful lasers—here’s what a shot day looks like

Ars Technica General Tech

The Texas Petawatt (TPW), a 17‑story‑deep, high‑power laser facility beneath the University of Texas at Austin’s Physics, Math, and Astronomy building, was a DOE‑funded research center that produced petawatt‑level pulses—brief bursts of light carrying more power than the U.S. electrical grid for a trillionth of a second—by stretching, amplifying, and then compressing laser pulses. As lead laser scientist from 2020‑2024, I oversaw “shot days” where targets ranging from ultra‑thin metal foils to gas jets were irradiated to probe stellar physics, fusion, and even medical applications. The process involved hours of meticulous preparation, culminating in a 10‑second window of near silence during the actual pulse, before the facility was shut down due to funding cuts.

There’s New Evidence for How Loneliness Affects Memory in Old Age

WIRED General Tech

A new six‑year longitudinal analysis of 10,217 European adults aged 65–94, published in *Aging & Mental Health*, shows that loneliness is linked to lower baseline memory performance—both immediate and delayed recall—but does not accelerate the rate of cognitive decline; age itself remains the strongest predictor of memory loss, with sharper drops after 75 and especially after 85, while depression, chronic illness, and loneliness further lower initial scores. Physical activity, however, lifts the baseline memory level, acting as a cognitive buffer without altering decline speed. Though the study does not pinpoint causality, it highlights loneliness as a significant factor in initial memory deficits, underscoring the need to address social isolation as part of strategies to mitigate cognitive decline in an aging population projected to grow substantially by 2050.
